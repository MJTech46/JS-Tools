The old lighthouse keeper believed that silence had a texture, something you could almost hold between your fingers if the wind stayed still long enough. Every evening he climbed the spiral stairs, counting each worn step out of habit rather than necessity, and watched the horizon dissolve into a soft, indigo haze. The sea rarely repeated itself. Some nights it moved like poured glass, unhurried and clear; other nights it churned with a restless, almost argumentative energy, flinging spray against the rocks below. He kept a leather journal where he recorded not the weather, but the mood of the water, describing it the way a portrait painter might describe a difficult, ever-changing subject. Visitors sometimes asked if the isolation troubled him. He would smile, gesture toward the endless expanse beyond the glass, and explain that solitude was simply company that asked nothing in return. The lamp above him turned patiently, throwing its beam across the waves, a quiet promise to distant sailors that somewhere, however small, a steady light was watching over their passage home.
